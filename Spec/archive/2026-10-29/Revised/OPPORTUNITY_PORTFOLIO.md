## How to choose

Prioritize access to a buyer and relevant evidence over how impressive an idea sounds. Each idea needs a repeated trigger, a measurable outcome, lawful inputs, and a person who can purchase. The suggested first-month tests are discovery tasks for a later approved phase, not actions already performed.

**Recommended order:** investigate A1 as the main product; evaluate B1/B2 as supporting capabilities; explore C1 as a differentiated research direction if the selected customer needs it. Hold the rest until customer access or evidence specifically favors them. If access is primarily to testing labs rather than implant manufacturers, A2/B3 may lead instead.

## A. Land and expand

### A1. Fixation design-family change evaluation — recommended first hypothesis

**Buyer/trigger:** a manufacturer verification lead revises a plate family or assembly and needs comparable evidence across declared variants.

**First product:** accept a baseline, one bounded design change, a fixed protocol and supported inputs; generate a reproducible baseline/candidate comparison, unresolved checks, and an engineering evidence bundle. Develop SDF-based analysis to reduce repeated geometry preparation; retain the tetra reference.

**Expansion:** recurring revisions, more variants within the qualified family, shared review, integration, then adjacent fixation families.

**Differentiator/moat candidate:** change-specific evidence plus an accepted family model and paired bench data. Generic FEA/optimization already exists; the opening is lower effort for this buyer's exact repeatable job.

**Obstacle:** the buyer may already have a scripted workflow or no recurring need. Model physics and commercial dependency rights must support the context.

**Revenue hypothesis:** $5k–$15k fixed-scope assisted pilot; $24k–$60k annual team workflow only after repeated use. Prices are test anchors.

**First-month kill test:** inspect at least three real change projects across independent buyers. If no budget owner can name a recent costly recurrence or provide lawful inputs, do not build a generic platform around this wedge.

### A2. A simulation/evidence workbench for mechanical testing labs

**Buyer/trigger:** a testing lab receives a new implant-variant campaign and must agree fixtures, specimens, comparisons, and reports.

**First product:** one lab's repeatable static fixture, linked model/test inputs, synchronized measurements, and traceable comparison report. Begin with existing solver outputs if useful; call that an explicit alternative commercial track if SDF is not part of delivery.

**Expansion:** more campaigns, customer review portal, calibration workflows, then additional test protocols.

**Moat candidate:** rights-cleared paired test/simulation data and embedding in recurring campaigns. Reuse rights must survive the lab's end-customer contracts.

**Obstacle:** test standards, hardware traceability, uncertain measurements, thin margins, and incumbent lab-management software. A lab may want engineering services rather than another system.

**Revenue hypothesis:** campaign-based fee first, then annual workflow license; determine price from real campaign labor and value.

**Kill test:** replay one historical campaign using customer-supplied records. If joining data and review does not save meaningful effort or repeat, deprioritize.

### A3. Embedded engineering evaluation for a CAD/personalization platform

**Buyer/trigger:** an upstream vendor wants analysis on a bounded implant template as part of its existing design workflow.

**First product:** an API for one supported design family, explicit model applicability and numerical state, structured results, and exportable evidence. Avoid a promise that arbitrary uploaded anatomy can be analyzed.

**Expansion:** additional templates, automated change events, larger enterprise accounts through the partner.

**Moat candidate:** distribution agreement plus validated integration and family evidence. An API contract alone offers weak protection; avoid dependence on one reseller.

**Obstacle:** upstream vendors may already have physics, can build it, or refuse to own result liability. This portfolio does not assume named CAD companies are interested customers.

**Revenue hypothesis:** paid integration plus annual minimum and usage band, after measured cost/support requirements.

**Kill test:** obtain a written technical gap and a budgeted integration owner. Generic enthusiasm for “AI simulation” is insufficient.

### A4. Design-envelope study planning and coverage review

**Buyer/trigger:** an R&D team plans which variants and fixtures to evaluate before a major physical-test campaign.

**First product:** define design variables and valid ranges, link evidence, expose uncovered combinations, select informative scenarios, and report worst observed conditions with explicit coverage limits.

**Expansion:** versioned design-family envelope, change-triggered studies, reviewer packages, integration with test planning.

**Moat candidate:** linked domain model, historical challenge cases, and accepted review protocols.

**Obstacle:** direct overlap with existing optimization and medical-simulation providers; a sampled maximum cannot become a global worst-case proof. Statistical coverage needs justified anatomy and parameter distributions.

**Revenue hypothesis:** paid study plus annual repeated-use contract, not per-patient pricing without demonstrated volume.

**Kill test:** ask reviewers to compare an actual existing study plan against an assisted coverage review. Stop if it creates no actionable change or its rationale is not trusted.

## B. Workflow automation

### B1. Design-change impact and evidence freshness

**Buyer/trigger:** a verification engineer receives a new CAD revision, material update, or load change and must identify what evidence is stale.

**First product:** explicit dependency rules linking inputs, protocols, runs, and reports; a change review lists affected artifacts and the reason. Humans approve scope before expensive reevaluation. Never infer unchanged mechanics from a small visual edit alone.

**Expansion:** CAD/PLM connections, repeatable review rules, multi-team change history.

**Moat candidate:** domain-specific dependency rules and institutional review context. Generic document diffing and lifecycle software are alternatives.

**Obstacle:** software integration and customer-specific process variance; “safe to reuse” is a consequential engineering claim requiring its own evidence.

**Revenue hypothesis:** a team subscription tied to changed-project volume. It may support the main product better than a standalone company.

**Kill test:** review ten historical changes with the responsible engineer. If rules cannot be made explicit or the existing workflow already tracks them well, do not automate this judgment.

### B2. Evidence package reconciliation and provenance

**Buyer/trigger:** a team prepares an internal design review or responds to a request to explain a calculation.

**First product:** reconcile geometry, load/material versions, solver logs, result tables, and report references; flag mismatches and missing provenance; assemble a review package with source links.

**Expansion:** quality-review workflows, standardized templates, submissions support under customer control.

**Moat candidate:** deep engineering artifact interpretation and adopted templates. A generic LLM report writer has little defensibility; calculations must remain sourced and deterministic.

**Obstacle:** compliance-document software already exists; proprietary formats and confidential inputs can complicate onboarding. No automated assertion of regulatory sufficiency.

**Revenue hypothesis:** fixed-scope package service, followed by recurring team usage if revisions are frequent.

**Kill test:** perform a manual audit of one real bundle. Measure errors caught and reviewer time saved; abandon a standalone product if value is merely prettier formatting.

### B3. Fixture/model calibration and test reconciliation

**Buyer/trigger:** a manufacturer or lab has simulation/experiment disagreement and repeatedly retunes models.

**First product:** align a controlled fixture and measurements with simulation inputs, separate fitted from held-out data, quantify residual discrepancies and uncertainty, preserve parameter provenance.

**Expansion:** recurring process/material changes, multiple test rigs, family-specific calibration libraries.

**Moat candidate:** paired data and practical knowledge of fixture/sensor/model discrepancies. Numerical fitting itself is widely available.

**Obstacle:** nonidentifiable parameters, poor measurement quality, expensive expert work, and the risk of fitting away the wrong physics. Cross-validation must be part of the product.

**Revenue hypothesis:** paid engineering engagement before a subscription. Potentially strong access route to paired evidence, but a services trap if little repeats.

**Kill test:** on a held-out fixture/configuration, do calibrated parameters improve the decision-relevant result? If they only reproduce the training curve, no predictive moat has been earned.

### B4. Registration and boundary-condition transfer with review

**Buyer/trigger:** an engineer repeats a supported analysis on another anatomy or revised geometry and redefines regions manually.

**First product:** propose correspondences for named load/support/attachment regions, show transfer quality and missing/ambiguous regions, require review before execution.

**Expansion:** one anatomy family, then supported deformities and interfaces with explicit rejection policies.

**Moat candidate:** rights-cleared expert registration corrections and domain-specific failure detection.

**Obstacle:** deformed anatomy can invalidate correspondences; ordinary surface geometry does not reveal nerves or tissue properties. Existing image/model tools provide automation alternatives.

**Revenue hypothesis:** bundled with repeated analysis; standalone value depends on frequency and time saved.

**Kill test:** blinded transfer review on held-out geometries. Measure expert correction time and dangerous missed failures, not just average landmark distance.

## C. Less conventional product/research directions

### C1. Choose the next measurement, not just the next design

**Buyer/trigger:** a team cannot make a robust design decision because load, attachment stiffness, material properties, or geometry are poorly known.

**First product:** for one declared engineering question, rank which feasible bench measurement would most reduce decision uncertainty. Example: compare improving a displacement measurement with another material test rather than running hundreds of assumed stiffness combinations.

**Potential differentiator:** a model that knows when the useful next action is collecting evidence. Connect uncertainty sensitivity, measurement cost, and a testable decision criterion. This is a value-of-information hypothesis, not a claim of an invented mathematical field.

**Moat candidate:** accumulated links between assumptions, measurements, and subsequent decision outcomes.

**Obstacle:** requires defensible uncertainty and experiment models; otherwise it merely recommends whichever parameter is varied most. A qualified domain expert remains involved.

**Revenue hypothesis:** attach to a test-campaign planning engagement, then qualify repeat use.

**Kill test:** compare a prospective recommended measurement with the team's normal choice. If it does not improve uncertainty or save a meaningful test, keep it as research.

### C2. Search for designs that break a claimed envelope

**Buyer/trigger:** an engineering reviewer wants to challenge a design family before committing to a test plan.

**First product:** adversarial search across permitted geometry, placement, loads, and assumptions, seeking counterexamples or numerical unresolved regions. Preserve found failures and how they were generated.

**Potential differentiator:** prioritize falsifying a claimed boundary instead of producing a reassuring pass percentage. The reported result is “no counterexample found in this search,” never a proof of universal safety.

**Moat candidate:** a rights-cleared challenge-case library and the learned ability to expose useful failures.

**Obstacle:** adversarial search and robust optimization have existing alternatives; pathologically unrealistic cases can waste review time. Domain validity must constrain search.

**Revenue hypothesis:** paid challenge study, then recurring qualification/change review.

**Kill test:** on historical projects, identify a previously overlooked but physically plausible configuration that changes the test plan; verify it independently.

### C3. Manufacturing-aware mechanical sensitivity

**Buyer/trigger:** an additive-manufactured fixation design changes orientation, process, finishing, or supplier, raising questions about which tolerances matter mechanically.

**First product:** one family and process; evaluate measured dimensional/material variation under a fixed engineering protocol, linking actual inspection/test records to the modeled geometry.

**Potential differentiator:** analyze the manufactured variation rather than only nominal CAD. This complements the SDF representation but requires real process data.

**Moat candidate:** paired process–geometry–test evidence with reuse rights and repeated supplier/process evaluations.

**Obstacle:** fatigue, surface defects, anisotropy and process variability are difficult; the initial static model cannot claim lifetime prediction. Standards/process competence and validation funding are prerequisites.

**Revenue hypothesis:** process-change engineering campaign first; annual monitoring only if a recurring decision exists.

**Kill test:** demonstrate on held-out manufactured specimens that the added variation data improves a declared comparison beyond nominal CAD. Do not launch as fatigue prediction without separate evidence.

### C4. Mechanically relevant anatomy selection

**Buyer/trigger:** a team has access to many lawful geometries but a limited budget for detailed analysis or physical fixtures.

**First product:** select a small set that covers declared mechanical response features and known morphological variation, document what is omitted, compare against simple geometry-based sampling.

**Potential differentiator:** choose cases for the question's mechanics, rather than equating many scans with useful coverage. The method might combine simplified screening and detailed confirmation, with independent holdouts.

**Moat candidate:** anatomy-to-mechanical-response annotations and evidence that the selection avoids consequential misses.

**Obstacle:** representativeness, biased source datasets, response-model error, and direct competition from cohort vendors. Synthetic variation is not evidence of real population coverage.

**Revenue hypothesis:** feature of a study-planning product before a separate dataset business.

**Kill test:** compare held-out governing cases missed by this selection and by a simple baseline under the same budget. If the complex selector adds no decision value, use the simpler method.

## What not to pursue now

Do not begin full-body clinical digital twins, general autonomous surgical planning, fracture/lifetime guarantees, orthodontic biological outcome prediction, or a marketplace of patient data. These directions multiply physics, rights, validation, and distribution problems before the initial buyer is known.

The most promising combination is **A1 as the paid job, B1/B2 as the product workflow, and C1 or C2 as a later differentiated capability**. This remains one coherent product until repeated use earns expansion.
